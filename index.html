The big picture
Members Are Listening. APA Needs to Talk About What They Care About.
“APA members are informed, engaged, and vote.”
APA Section 6 Negotiations Messaging Guide (APA_Messaging_Guide_7/27/26), July 2026

Across every tab, one pattern stands out. APA has the public’s attention, but it isn’t yet giving members what they want to hear. APA’s leaders are well covered and public tone improved as Section 6 opened. Inside the membership, the most engaged pilots are focused on scheduling and APU, doubt the bargaining process and want more detail than they are getting. Members are still reading: they come to alliedpilots.org for contract and scheduling material, and they respond to content about people. The opportunity is to point APA’s communication at what pilots care about and deliver it where they already look.

October tested that. On Oct 9 APA posted the Board’s first Section 6 update, which addressed APU directly, but it didn’t reach Facebook or Instagram, which carried one post between Oct 6 and 20, about Chuck Yeager. (Email and the Information Hotline aren’t measured in this report.) The press didn’t cover Section 6 at all from Oct 6 to 26, and forum reaction to the APU news was skeptical. The October 2026 UNH poll, fielded Oct 5–17, confirms it from the whole membership, not just the forum. Members’ positive view of APA fell from 54% to 47%, the lowest since 2024, and “poor contract negotiations” became the reason for nearly a third of negative views. 35% trust APA to negotiate an effective contract and 41% don’t. Board communication held at 57%, so the problem isn’t that members hear too little; it’s that what they hear hasn’t built confidence in the bargaining. Only half have seen APA’s Section 6 and Railway Labor Act explainers, and 32% didn’t know they existed. Earlier polling adds two lessons: members trust their domicile reps (84%) more than National Officers (67–68%), and in December 2022 about a quarter got most of their information on the tentative agreement from word of mouth, a leaked copy, the forum or social media.

The last week of the period showed where members go when APA is quiet. Section 6 threads drew 13 posts from 12 pilots in Oct 21–26 and APU threads six, while the forum’s attention moved to a doubling of premiums for APA-sponsored disability insurance (106 posts, much of it aimed at APA) and a former American first officer’s video about quitting (182 posts). In that thread, members noted that no one from the company or the union contacted him when he resigned, and that pilots increasingly get their information from podcasts, YouTube and outside Facebook groups. Publicly, the conversation around American’s pilots was driven by the company’s Oct 22 results: record revenue and a loss on fuel costs, the backdrop management is likely to bring to the table. Capt. Tajer answered in The Dallas Morning News and Forbes. APA posted the Negotiating Committee’s next update, on Section 6 and interest-based bargaining, on Oct 27; it reached the website but not Facebook or Instagram, and drew no press. The week’s most-engaged social post was APA congratulating American’s dispatchers on ratifying their contract (1,799 engagements), and the most-covered APA story was an Aeromedical–HIMS Committee warning about a crew-meal dessert that reached aviation blogs through an outside account and was then reported retracted.

Across the two months the contrast is stark. September had 32 social posts, 98 press placements built around APA’s leaders and a forum focused on APU and the committee. October had 10 social posts, 103 placements that were mostly one syndicated Spirit story, and a forum whose attention drifted to benefits costs and a public resignation once contract news went quiet. Outside APA, no legacy airline has set a new pilot benchmark yet (Peer Airlines tab): the newest deals are Allegiant’s roughly 40% raise and PSA’s Nov 4 extension to 2030, and Delta, amendable Dec 31, is the next one to watch.

Section 6 ends with a member vote, so this matters more than press coverage. Last round, the busiest months on the forum came right after a document landed, when members were reacting before they fully understood it. Rebuilding confidence
How APA Can Rebuild Confidence in the Bargaining

The October poll shows the problem is confidence in the bargaining, not how often APA talks to members: trust that APA can negotiate an effective contract fell from 41% to 35%, with 41% now disagreeing, while the Board’s communication rating held at 57%. Saying that confidence has dropped won’t restore it. Showing progress, the people doing the work and how the process works will. The results will circulate after the Board briefing, so APA should have a short holding statement ready that points to what it is doing, but its public answer should be action, not the numbers.

01
Make APU the first visible proof that this approach works
The APU agreement, as briefed to Allyn on Oct 28, puts seniority back into APU, something American didn’t have to give. Once the Board has acted, tell it as members’ own story: pilots raised the problem, the Board tasked the Negotiating Committee, and the committee delivered. Pair it with one unified Board message backing the committee. This depends on the Board’s Nov 2–6 decision, which isn’t known yet. If APU goes to a member vote and fails, it becomes the opposite proof point, so prepare for both outcomes.
02
Explain interest-based bargaining before members judge it
With no list of demands, members have nothing to measure progress against, and the Oct 28 meeting confirmed many pilots don’t understand the approach. Explain in plain language why there is no demand list, what members get instead and how APA will report progress. Put it on the Section 6 site, in the News Digest and on social, because 32% of members didn’t know APA’s explainers existed and only 27% of pilots aged 21 to 30 have seen any.
03
Answer the competence question with people
Distrust in APA’s negotiating competence is members’ top concern about opening Scope (19%), and the forum keeps asking about the committee’s experience. “Meet the Negotiators” is APA’s best-performing Section 6 content, yet the third profile never reached social. Continue the series and add who supports the committee: attorneys, analysts and experienced advisors.
04
Give a regular rhythm with real substance
Set a dated update cadence and keep a simple tracker on the Section 6 site showing which sections are open, which are agreed in principle and what comes next. The Sept 18 update was the most-read APA communication on the forum and was still called thin. Progress members can count builds confidence faster than reassurance does.
05
Brief domicile reps first
Members find domicile reps far more credible (84%) than National Officers (67–68%, 2024 polling). Give reps the same talking points the day before each update so the most trusted voices carry APA’s account, and log what they hear back using the Sentiment Framework tab.
06
Close loops in public
“Lack of representation and listening” is the second most common reason for negative views (20%). Explain the disability premium increase, which APA’s benefits committee attributes to claims usage set by the actuary, and post a monthly “you asked, here’s the answer” drawn from the forum’s top questions, answered for everyone through official channels.
07
Speak to junior and senior pilots separately
73% of pilots aged 21 to 30 prefer a broader, longer negotiation, against 16% of those 61 and older, and support for “spreading the wealth” fell most among both groups. Each major update should say what the approach means for each, so neither reads it as written for the other.
08
Get ahead of peer deals
Prepare responses now on Allegiant’s roughly 40% raise, PSA’s frozen-pay extension and Delta, whose contract becomes amendable Dec 31 (Peer Airlines tab). Last round, United’s deal reset member expectations and led to a postponed vote.
How we’ll know it’s working: agreement that APA can negotiate an effective contract back above disagreement in the next UNH wave; more members reporting they have seen the Section 6 explainers; a falling share of forum negotiation posts about process and trust; and steady visits to the Section 6 site between updates.
Social media
What APA Should Do on Social

Facebook and Instagram are where APA can reach members without a login or an email open, and the data shows what works there: people, not process. In September, when APA posted 32 times around 9/11, member events and the first negotiator profile, its posts drew 32,613 engagements. In October it posted 10 times and drew 4,367. These recommendations turn social into a steady, trusted source on Section 6.

01
Never go dark on Section 6
October had 10 Facebook and Instagram posts, down from 32 in September, and none on Section 6 after Oct 2. When APA goes quiet, the forum fills the gap. Set a floor of at least one Section 6 post a week, even when the only news is that talks continue, and never let two weeks pass without one.
02
Every member update goes to social the same day
The Oct 9 Board update, the Oct 16 “Meet the Negotiators” profile and the Oct 27 committee update never reached Facebook or Instagram. Make social part of the release checklist: when something posts to alliedpilots.org or goes out in the News Digest, a social version goes out the same day.
03
Lead with people, not process
People content performs best: member events and new-hire posts averaged about 1,460 engagements each, “Meet the Negotiators” drew 33% and 22% engagement rates on Facebook, and the Oct 28 post congratulating American’s dispatchers was October’s best (1,799 engagements). Process-only posts such as bidding alerts averaged about 73. Put a face on every Section 6 message: a negotiator, a domicile rep or a member.
04
Turn the News Digest into a weekly carousel
Pull three or four highlights from each Digest into a short carousel with a consistent “APA Digest” look and link it to the Section 6 site (in the bio on Instagram, directly on Facebook). Social sends only about 500–600 site visits a month, so this is the path from scrolling to the full story. Tag every link so the Website Analytics tab can show which posts send members to the site.
05
Explain in short formats
Turn the Railway Labor Act, interest-based bargaining and Scope explainers into short reels and three- to five-slide carousels. Younger pilots are the least likely to have seen the explainers and the most likely to be on Instagram, where cross-posted Facebook content does poorly (median engagement rate 2.7% in October against 13.2% on Facebook). Build Instagram versions for Instagram.
06
Show proof, not promises
Use a simple “you said, we did” format for outcomes such as the APU fix once the Board has acted, and post the Section 6 tracker as a recurring graphic when a section moves. Members will believe progress they can see.
07
Let domicile reps carry the message
Domiciles already publish more than half of alliedpilots.org’s articles and are members’ most trusted voices. Feature reps in short videos explaining each major update in their own words, alongside the national post.
08
Bring public wins home
Capt. Tajer’s comments on American’s results in The Dallas Morning News and Forbes, the Senate mental-health bills and the Spirit data objection never reached APA’s social channels. Share earned coverage with a one-line member takeaway, so members hear it from APA first.
09
Engage, don’t debate
Answer factual questions in comments with links to the official source, correct misinformation through the rapid-response process, and don’t argue with critics in public threads. Members say they increasingly get information from podcasts, YouTube and outside Facebook groups, so APA’s pages need to be the reliable, consistent source.
10
Close the measurement gaps
LinkedIn isn’t connected to Hootsuite Analytics, and APA’s X and YouTube channels aren’t tracked. Connecting them lets this report measure every channel. Track engagement per Section 6 post and clicks to the Section 6 site as the two headline social measures.

Have a Delta response ready. Delta’s pilot contract becomes amendable Dec 31 and its talks have stalled over scheduling, the issue 62% of APA members rank above pay. Last round, United’s deal reset expectations and led to a postponed vote (Peer Airlines tab). Draft the explanation of how any Delta deal relates to APA’s talks before it lands.

What it means for this round
A quiet forum is normal at this stage. 66 pilots in negotiation threads matches every early or between-documents year.
A document changes everything. The 2022 tentative agreement brought six to seven times the usual number of pilots into the conversation. That is the moment APA’s own explanation most needs to arrive first.
What to watch
A jump without a document. If negotiation threads climb well past 80 pilots before there is anything to react to, read it as frustration, not information.

Lessons
What Last Round Tells Us About This One
01
The forum reads more negative than the membership
At the end, the forum’s exit poll had 56% yes among pilots who said how they voted. The real result was 72.7% yes. Earlier, TA 1.0 polled 85% no on the forum. Use the forum to see which issues are live, not to forecast a vote.
02
Trust in the process was the steadiest theme
Posts about the Negotiating Committee, the Board and union leadership ran at 12–18% of negotiation posts for most of the round, about as often as pay. In September 2026 they are 23%, higher than in any phase of the last round.
03
Solidarity fights come back
About 3,400 posts last round argued over pilots, and union leaders, picking up premium flying while APA sought leverage. APU is this round’s version of that fight.
04
Peer deals set expectations
Mentions of Delta and United climbed once their deals landed, and United’s July 2023 agreement led to the postponed vote and TA 2.0. Expect the next Delta or United contract to reset what members see as good enough.
05
Peaks follow documents
The three busiest months each followed something members could read: July 2023 (a TA, then its revision), May 2023 (an AIP) and August 2023 (the ratification vote on the revised TA). Communication plans should be ready before each one lands, not after.

For APA
What This Means for Member Expectations
01
Allegiant’s number will travel
“Forty percent now” is easy to repeat and will likely show up on the forum and in social posts as a yardstick. Allegiant started from much lower pay, so the percentage isn’t comparable. Have a short explanation ready that compares pay rates, not percentages.
02
PSA shows a deal isn’t automatically a win
The PSA extension trades a pay freeze and some concessions for certainty to 2030, and it was approved without a member vote. Members may cite it either as evidence that leaders sign deals without asking pilots or as a sign of what American wants from its pilot groups. APA’s own process, with a member ratification vote, is the contrast worth stating.
03
Delta is the deal to prepare for
Last round, United’s July 2023 agreement led directly to APA’s postponed vote and TA 2.0 (Section 6 tab). Delta’s talks are the most likely next legacy benchmark. Its public dispute is over scheduling, the same issue 62% of APA members say matters more than pay. Have a response ready for whenever Delta reaches a tentative agreement.
04
Labor wins at American perform well with members
APA’s Oct 28 post congratulating American’s dispatchers on ratifying their contract drew 1,799 engagements on Facebook, the most of any October post (Social Media tab). Recognizing other work groups’ wins, and the turnout behind them, is content members respond to.

01
APU is seen as a seniority issue, and as APA’s to fix
Posters describe APU as a loss of seniority rights, not a scheduling glitch. Recurring asks: clear guidance on what’s allowed, enforcement against pilots who break the rules or use bots, and a visible fix. Several posters question why the same Negotiating Committee and JIAC members who implemented APU are now tasked with fixing it. Others worry a fix will cost concessions in Section 6. The most-discussed practical idea is running a seniority ballot before a trip drops into APU.
02
Confidence in the Negotiating Committee is low among posters
A 71-post thread argued the Negotiating Committee and JIAC should be returned to the line. Criticism centers on the committee being junior and all narrowbody, on calls for professional negotiators and on whether the Board will reaffirm the committee at its fall meeting (Nov 2–6). A few posters raised recall petitions. Some members pushed back and defended the committee, and one noted that APA already has staff attorney negotiators.
03
The Sept 18 bargaining update drew skepticism, but also ideas
The Negotiating Committee’s update on initial bargaining and the NMB was the most-read APA communication on the forum. Reactions called it light on detail. Members worried the company will seek concessions on qualifications, training and seniority-based awards. Others argued the company’s training and staffing pinch gives pilots leverage, and posted specific Section 6 asks on work rules and pay protection.
04
The CEO critique landed with mixed reviews
Some posters welcomed APA being vocal about American’s leadership. Others doubted a board seat would give pilots real influence, argued APA should fix its own house first, or asked why there hasn’t been a formal vote of no confidence.
05
A senior-versus-junior divide runs through everything
Senior posters say APA works for the junior majority and discounts their history, including in replies to the President’s Labor Day message. That lines up with the September 2025 poll, where junior pilots were far more supportive of “spreading the wealth” on premium flying and of holding out for a bigger contract. The October 2026 poll shows the same split on timing: 73% of pilots aged 21 to 30 prefer a broader, longer negotiation, against 16% of those 61 and older.
06
Early October: scope, experience and time at headquarters
Three threads drove early October. American’s announcement that Alaska will join its Atlantic and Pacific joint businesses drew 45 posts, mostly about whether contract scope protects American pilots’ widebody flying, a Section 6 issue. The second “Meet the Negotiators” profile (Oct 2) prompted questions about negotiators’ major-airline experience, alongside posts wishing the committee well. A new thread questioned how much time Negotiating Committee members spend at APA headquarters.
07
Oct 6–20: the APU announcement, a base resolution and forum moderation
After the Board said on Oct 9 that APU changes are being pursued outside Section 6, APU posters were skeptical: several expect the fix to cost pilots something, asked who is accountable for how APU was agreed, and some suggested retired negotiators advise the committee. A DFW domicile resolution to explore an Austin/San Antonio co-terminal or satellite base during Section 6 drew 71 posts, mostly opposed to a co-terminal. APA’s Oct 14 update on the Alaska/Hawaiian scope grievance prompted debate over how clear the contract’s scope language is. The third “Meet the Negotiators” profile again raised questions about committee members’ widebody and major-airline experience. The largest new thread outside contract topics protested the forum’s suspension of a long-time poster; posters across viewpoints called it heavy-handed, which feeds the trust concerns on this tab. A 182-post thread debated proposals to raise the pilot retirement age.
08
Oct 21–26: benefits costs, a public resignation and scope
The week’s largest thread reacted to a video by a former American 787 first officer explaining why he quit. Many posters were dismissive of the video, but a recurring point was that no one from the company or the union contacted him when he resigned, and several noted that pilots now get much of their information from podcasts, YouTube and outside Facebook groups. The second-largest thread, from 31 pilots, was anger at another doubling of premiums for APA-sponsored disability and loss-of-license insurance. Some blamed APA’s management of the plans; others tied the rise to more claims since the 2023 contract and the age-65 retirement rule, and several asked how to compare or cancel coverage. The DFW Austin/San Antonio resolution drew 63 more posts, still mostly opposed. Smaller threads argued that JetBlue’s financial trouble could end the domestic codeshare that the current scope language allows, and that the next contract should close that gap, and a senior pilot’s post about heavy trip reassignments asked for stronger work rules, with replies doubting APA will set the industry standard this time. Section 6 threads themselves were quiet: 13 negotiation posts from 12 pilots.

A thread on American’s projected loss for the year (84 posts) argued over what the company’s finances mean for bargaining, and the disability premium thread continued, with posters linking higher claims to the age-65 rule and asking about enrollment in the related loss-of-license plan.

Sentiment Framework
APA Member Sentiment Aggregation Framework
Proposed · for APA leadership review
Allyn, “APA Member Sentiment Aggregation Framework” (working document) · benchmark figures refreshed to this report’s September–October data
Framework
Purpose

APA leadership should read informal channels for direction and timing, and read scientific polling for size. Informal channels can move in the same direction as the official polls, but the Allyn Insights report shows they don’t match them in level. They skew negative and are driven by a small group. The member forum, The Line, reflects about 2.4% of 16,000 members, its top 10 posters wrote 38% of posts and its exit poll ran about 17 points more negative than the 2023 ratification result.

This framework does two things. It brings every member’s touchpoint into one consistent trend picture. It also sets decision rules so leadership acts on confirmed shifts, not on the loudest inbound message that week.

The goal ties back to the program’s core objective: members who are informed, engaged and ready to vote. This system prevents overreaction, but it won’t close the trust gap on its own. The October UNH poll put trust in APA to negotiate an effective contract at 35%, and the Insights report concludes the drop is about bargaining, not communication.

Counting and measurement rules:

Count unique pilots, not messages. Ten emails from one pilot is one data point.
Weight by base size so one vocal base doesn’t look like the whole membership.
Track concentration: report the share of posts from the top 10 contributors. In September they wrote 53% of negotiation posts, against 21–28% last round.
Keep trend queries fixed. A query that changes between pulls breaks the trend line, so compare only within one query version.
Have a person review automated sentiment before it reaches leadership. Use the tighter Hootsuite measure (mentions that include pilots) as the headline tone metric, as the Insights report recommends.

Framework
Response rule: fast on facts, slow on positions

This is the main safeguard against knee-jerk reactions. Correcting a fact and changing course are two separate tracks with two different speeds.

TrackWhat triggers itResponseSpeed
Fix the factsMisinformation or a factual error in circulationUse the rapid-response library and escalation protocolsSame day where possible

Emotional volume can trigger a check. It can’t by itself trigger a change of course.

Framework
Reporting cadence

Leadership gets a short weekly brief, not the raw feed. Each layer checks the one below it against a more reliable source.

CadenceDeliverableWhat it checks
WeeklyOne-page trend brief: top issues, what’s rising, what’s fading, anything that met the escalation rulesInformal channels and online sentiment
MonthlyReconciliation noteDo the weekly trends match the pulse surveys?
QuarterlyCalibration reviewDid the informal channels predict how the scientific poll moved?

The weekly briefs feed the Allyn Insights report, which covers about eight weeks at a time, so the two don’t duplicate each other. The quarterly review tests whether informal channels track the official polls, using evidence instead of impressions. The October UNH poll (fielded Oct 5–17, 903 interviews) is the first calibration point. Over time the review shows which channels deserve more weight and which mostly add noise.

Framework
Governance and confidentiality

The system only works if leadership agrees to the rules before the next spike, not during it.

One owner handles intake and coding for every channel.
Leadership agrees ahead of time that no position changes because of raw inbound volume alone.
Each decision gets logged with the evidence behind it, which also feeds the quarterly calibration.
Sound-off content is reported only in aggregate, with no names attached. If pilots believe their sound-offs reach a leadership dashboard with their names on them, they’ll likely stop being candid and the early-warning value is lost.
Brief domicile reps before each major update. They’re the most trusted messengers (84%) and can report back what they hear at their base.
