The big picture
Members Are Listening. APA Needs to Talk About What They Care About.
“APA members are informed, engaged, and vote.”
APA Section 6 Negotiations Messaging Guide (APA_Messaging_Guide_7/27/26), July 2026

Across every tab, one pattern stands out. APA has the public’s attention, but it isn’t yet giving members what they want to hear. APA’s leaders are well covered and public tone improved as Section 6 opened. Inside the membership, the most engaged pilots are focused on scheduling and APU, doubt the bargaining process and want more detail than they are getting. Members are still reading: they come to alliedpilots.org for contract and scheduling material, and they respond to content about people. The opportunity is to point APA’s communication at what pilots care about and deliver it where they already look.

October tested that. On Oct 9 APA posted the Board’s first Section 6 update, which addressed APU directly, but it didn’t reach Facebook or Instagram, which carried one post between Oct 6 and 20, about Chuck Yeager. (Email and the Information Hotline aren’t measured in this report.) The press didn’t cover Section 6 at all from Oct 6 to 26, and forum reaction to the APU news was skeptical. Polling going back to 2021 points the same way: members rate APA’s value higher than ever (81%), but their rating of Board communication fell from 77% to 58% in one wave, they trust their domicile reps (84%) more than National Officers (67–68%), and in December 2022 about a quarter got most of their information on the tentative agreement from word of mouth, a leaked copy, the forum or social media.

The last week of the period showed where members go when APA is quiet. Section 6 threads drew 13 posts from 12 pilots in Oct 21–26 and APU threads six, while the forum’s attention moved to a doubling of premiums for APA-sponsored disability insurance (106 posts, much of it aimed at APA) and a former American first officer’s video about quitting (182 posts). In that thread, members noted that no one from the company or the union contacted him when he resigned, and that pilots increasingly get their information from podcasts, YouTube and outside Facebook groups. Publicly, the conversation around American’s pilots was driven by the company’s Oct 22 results: record revenue and a loss on fuel costs, the backdrop management is likely to bring to the table. Capt. Tajer answered in The Dallas Morning News and Forbes, the only new press naming APA that week. APA posted the Negotiating Committee’s next update, on Section 6 and interest-based bargaining, on Oct 27, one day after this period; member reaction will show in the November update.

Section 6 ends with a member vote, so this matters more than press coverage. Last round, the busiest months on the forum came right after a document landed, when members were reacting before they fully understood it. Getting results
What to Do, and When

The takeaways above turn into a short list of actions. They are grouped by timing, because the next few weeks include the Board’s Nov 2–6 meeting and the 2026 poll results, both of which will shape how members judge the start of talks.

Now through the Board meeting (Nov 2–6)
Take the Oct 9 APU update to every channel. It didn’t reach Facebook or Instagram. Put it on social, in email and in domicile reps’ hands, and follow it with specifics: what changes, when and how seniority is protected. Forum posters expect the fix to cost something, so say plainly what it does and doesn’t trade.
Restart social for Section 6, starting with the Oct 27 update. Facebook and Instagram carried two posts from Oct 6 to 26 (Chuck Yeager and a safety summit), and the third “Meet the Negotiators” profile (Oct 16) didn’t reach either. The Oct 27 committee update on interest-based bargaining is the next test; the series is APA’s best-performing Section 6 content. The series is APA’s best-performing Section 6 content.
Set a bargaining update rhythm. Commit to a regular date, and use each update to cover where talks stand, what can’t be shared and why, and what comes next.
Explain the disability insurance increase. 31 pilots spent the last week of October arguing about why premiums doubled, and some blamed APA. A short, plain explanation of why rates rose and what members’ options are would take the question off the forum and show APA answering before it is asked twice.
Carry APA’s read of American’s finances to members. American reported record revenue and a loss on fuel costs on Oct 22, and management is likely to bring that to the table. APA already made its case in the press (Capt. Tajer in The Dallas Morning News and Forbes) and in an Oct 26 Investor Relations article, but not on Facebook or Instagram. Put it where members scroll, in plain terms, before the company’s version becomes the accepted one.
Prepare for the Board’s decisions. Draft the member explanation of anything the Board decides about the Negotiating Committee, so members hear it from APA before the forum interprets it.
Through the end of the year
Build a Section 6 home on alliedpilots.org. One page with every bargaining update in order, tagged consistently, and linked from every social post and email.
Answer the experience question. Continue “Meet the Negotiators” and add who supports the committee and how the team works.
Explain scope before it becomes a fight. A short explainer on how the current contract protects American pilots’ flying, building on the Oct 14 Alaska/Hawaiian grievance update, and a clear answer on what the DFW Austin/San Antonio resolution would and wouldn’t mean.
Bring public wins home. Share earned moments, such as the Senate bills and the Spirit objection, with members on APA’s own channels.
Every month until ratification
Arm domicile reps first. They are the most credible source members have. Brief them before each major update so they can answer questions in crew rooms the same day.
Use the forum as a question list, not a vote count. Each month, pull the top member questions and answer them for everyone through official channels. Don’t debate posters or match their tone.
Have materials ready before each document lands. Explainers, FAQs and site pages for each tentative agreement or major milestone, ready before release.
Plan for peer deals. When Delta or United reaches a new pilot contract, have a response ready that explains how it relates to APA’s talks.
Write for senior and junior pilots alike. Show what each group gains from APA’s priorities and timing, so neither reads a message as written for the other.

For three years the conversation ran at a few hundred to about 1,000 posts a month. It jumped when there was something concrete to react to. The October 2022 tentative agreement, which the Board rejected on Nov 2, drew 5,666 posts. The May 2023 Agreement in Principle drew 6,998. July 2023, when a TA went to members, the vote was postponed days after United’s pilots reached their own deal, and TA 2.0 followed, drew 9,808 posts from 770 pilots. That was the busiest month of the round, followed by May 2023 and the August 2023 ratification vote (6,436).

Lessons
What Last Round Tells Us About This One
01
The forum reads more negative than the membership
At the end, the forum’s exit poll had 56% yes among pilots who said how they voted. The real result was 72.7% yes. Earlier, TA 1.0 polled 85% no on the forum. Use the forum to see which issues are live, not to forecast a vote.
02
Trust in the process was the steadiest theme
Posts about the Negotiating Committee, the Board and union leadership ran at 12–18% of negotiation posts for most of the round, about as often as pay. In September 2026 they are 23%, higher than in any phase of the last round.
03
Solidarity fights come back
About 3,400 posts last round argued over pilots, and union leaders, picking up premium flying while APA sought leverage. APU is this round’s version of that fight.
04
Peer deals set expectations
Mentions of Delta and United climbed once their deals landed, and United’s July 2023 agreement led to the postponed vote and TA 2.0. Expect the next Delta or United contract to reset what members see as good enough.
05
Peaks follow documents
The three busiest months each followed something members could read: July 2023 (a TA, then its revision), May 2023 (an AIP) and August 2023 (the ratification vote on the revised TA). Communication plans should be ready before each one lands, not after.

Themes
What Members Are Saying
01
APU is seen as a seniority issue, and as APA’s to fix
Posters describe APU as a loss of seniority rights, not a scheduling glitch. Recurring asks: clear guidance on what’s allowed, enforcement against pilots who break the rules or use bots, and a visible fix. Several posters question why the same Negotiating Committee and JIAC members who implemented APU are now tasked with fixing it. Others worry a fix will cost concessions in Section 6. The most-discussed practical idea is running a seniority ballot before a trip drops into APU.
02
Confidence in the Negotiating Committee is low among posters
A 71-post thread argued the Negotiating Committee and JIAC should be returned to the line. Criticism centers on the committee being junior and all narrowbody, on calls for professional negotiators and on whether the Board will reaffirm the committee at its fall meeting (Nov 2–6). A few posters raised recall petitions. Some members pushed back and defended the committee, and one noted that APA already has staff attorney negotiators.
03
The Sept 18 bargaining update drew skepticism, but also ideas
The Negotiating Committee’s update on initial bargaining and the NMB was the most-read APA communication on the forum. Reactions called it light on detail. Members worried the company will seek concessions on qualifications, training and seniority-based awards. Others argued the company’s training and staffing pinch gives pilots leverage, and posted specific Section 6 asks on work rules and pay protection.
04
The CEO critique landed with mixed reviews
Some posters welcomed APA being vocal about American’s leadership. Others doubted a board seat would give pilots real influence, argued APA should fix its own house first, or asked why there hasn’t been a formal vote of no confidence.
05
A senior-versus-junior divide runs through everything
Senior posters say APA works for the junior majority and discounts their history, including in replies to the President’s Labor Day message. That lines up with the September 2025 poll, where junior pilots were far more supportive of “spreading the wealth” on premium flying and of holding out for a bigger contract.
06
Early October: scope, experience and time at headquarters
Three threads drove early October. American’s announcement that Alaska will join its Atlantic and Pacific joint businesses drew 45 posts, mostly about whether contract scope protects American pilots’ widebody flying, a Section 6 issue. The second “Meet the Negotiators” profile (Oct 2) prompted questions about negotiators’ major-airline experience, alongside posts wishing the committee well. A new thread questioned how much time Negotiating Committee members spend at APA headquarters.
07
Oct 6–20: the APU announcement, a base resolution and forum moderation
After the Board said on Oct 9 that APU changes are being pursued outside Section 6, APU posters were skeptical: several expect the fix to cost pilots something, asked who is accountable for how APU was agreed, and some suggested retired negotiators advise the committee. A DFW domicile resolution to explore an Austin/San Antonio co-terminal or satellite base during Section 6 drew 71 posts, mostly opposed to a co-terminal. APA’s Oct 14 update on the Alaska/Hawaiian scope grievance prompted debate over how clear the contract’s scope language is. The third “Meet the Negotiators” profile again raised questions about committee members’ widebody and major-airline experience. The largest new thread outside contract topics protested the forum’s suspension of a long-time poster; posters across viewpoints called it heavy-handed, which feeds the trust concerns on this tab. A 182-post thread debated proposals to raise the pilot retirement age.
08
Oct 21–26: benefits costs, a public resignation and scope
The week’s largest thread reacted to a video by a former American 787 first officer explaining why he quit. Many posters were dismissive of the video, but a recurring point was that no one from the company or the union contacted him when he resigned, and several noted that pilots now get much of their information from podcasts, YouTube and outside Facebook groups. The second-largest thread, from 31 pilots, was anger at another doubling of premiums for APA-sponsored disability and loss-of-license insurance. Some blamed APA’s management of the plans; others tied the rise to more claims since the 2023 contract and the age-65 retirement rule, and several asked how to compare or cancel coverage. The DFW Austin/San Antonio resolution drew 63 more posts, still mostly opposed. Smaller threads argued that JetBlue’s financial trouble could end the domestic codeshare that the current scope language allows, and that the next contract should close that gap, and a senior pilot’s post about heavy trip reassignments asked for stronger work rules, with replies doubting APA will set the industry standard this time. Section 6 threads themselves were quiet: 13 negotiation posts from 12 pilots.

Takeaways
What the Survey Tells Us
01
Members value APA, but feel less informed by the Board
81% said APA’s services and representation are a very good or good value. But only 58% rated the Board of Directors’ communication and keeping members informed as very good or good, down 19 points from 77% in February 2025. That rating fell in every age, base, seat and seniority group UNH reported. It is the survey’s most direct measure of the “informed” half of APA’s objective.
02
Overall sentiment is recovering but hasn’t returned to 2024 levels
51% viewed APA very positively or positively, up from 48% in February 2025 but below 60% in February 2024. 18% were negative. Among negative respondents, the leading reasons were poor contract negotiations (23%), “corruption and self-serving leadership” (19%) and lack of representation and listening to members (15%). Among positive respondents, the top reason was the union’s focus on pilot interests (21%).
03
Scheduling, not pay rates, is the top contract priority
60% named scheduling rules as the most important thing to improve in bargaining, and 80% ranked it first or second. Compensation was first for 18% (46% first or second). Within scheduling, improved pairing quality led at 47%. Within pay, trip and duty rigs led at 38% (61% first or second), ahead of hourly rates (26%).
04
Members want a better deal over a faster one
55% disagreed that it is better to reach a deal sooner with fewer changes (29% agreed). 73% disagreed with securing a contract first so APA can re-enter talks sooner “even if it means we set the market,” and 61% agreed it is better to let other carriers ratify first. Junior pilots hold this view most strongly: 76% of 31-to-40-year-olds rejected a faster, smaller deal, compared with 36% of pilots 61 and older.
05
Support for “spreading the wealth” on premium flying is rising
64% agreed with scheduling changes that would “spread the wealth,” such as Seniority in Turn for Premium, up from 49% in September 2024. Agreement that the most senior pilots should always get first opportunity at premium sequences slipped from 59% to 53%. This is the fault line behind Aggressive Pick-Up, and it splits by seniority.

What else the 2024 waves showed
Members trust their local reps more than national leaders. 84% found information from domicile representatives credible in both 2024 waves, against 67–68% for National Officers. 76% rated domicile reps good at keeping them informed (February 2024).
Confidence in the Board was low. Two-thirds had only some or little confidence in the Board in both 2024 waves, even while 66% rated its communication good.

Who is least satisfied
Mid-career pilots and some bases. In both 2024 waves, pilots with 11–20 years of service and Phoenix-based pilots were the most negative about APA, the Board and APA’s effort in the last Section 6 talks. These are small groups, so read them as direction.
Last round’s effort still shapes views. In February 2024, 65% rated APA’s effort in the last Section 6 negotiations good, but only 33% of pilots with 11–20 years did. Unhappiness with the contract or negotiations was among the leading reasons for negative views in each wave from 2024 to 2025.

What it means for 2026
Frustration with APA peaked as a TA got close. In 2022, more than a quarter of members gave “APA hasn’t produced” as their main reason for wanting to explore a merger. Data quality
Read These Numbers With Care
01
The prior period includes traffic that doesn’t look like members
Several summer landing pages drew one-page, near-zero-engagement visits: News (15,387 sessions, 22% engaged), Spouse Support Network (3,275, 6%), Careers (3,174, 4%) and Gone West (2,275, 2%). The prior period also logged 24,394 form starts from 24,129 users, compared with 5,242 since Sept 1. That pattern suggests automated or accidental traffic, but GA4 alone can’t confirm it. The engaged-session count, down 8% (120,453 vs. 131,316), is a fairer comparison than raw sessions.
02
Page views are inflated by the News page
As reported through Oct 5, visits that start on the News page log about 41 page views each, which points to an auto-refresh or tracking issue. Page views are left out of this report until IT checks it.
03
Two other GA4 properties are connected
AgencyAnalytics also has two small properties with the same page structure as alliedpilots.org. They may be the test sites found in Google (SEO tab), but that isn’t confirmed. They are excluded here. 12,325 sessions with no landing page recorded, at 3% engagement, are included in the totals.
